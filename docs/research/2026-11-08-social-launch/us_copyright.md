# Fork social launch: US recipe copyright and platform exposure

Research/access date: **8 November 2026**. US scope only; Canada is being researched separately. Fork's operator/legal entity and its location remain unconfirmed. This is decision support for product planning, not a determination of liability for a specific recipe. No terms were drafted, registrations made, or third parties contacted.

## 1. If a user posts a professional chef's recipe as their own, what is the risk?

### Takeaway

**Yes, Fork can face a complaint or claim; it is not automatically liable simply because a user posted it. What was copied and what Fork did matter.** A cook can reproduce a cooking method without owning the chef's expressive writing or photograph. Fork needs both an attribution policy and an operational copyright process; credit alone does not authorize copying.

### Cited Findings

- The Copyright Office distinguishes unprotectable ingredient lists and basic procedures from protectable creative explanations, illustrations and photographs. A cookbook's sufficiently creative selection/arrangement can also be protected even when its individual facts are not. The chef's professional status does not change that distinction. [Copyright Office Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)
- Copyright includes rights to reproduce, adapt, distribute and publicly display protected expression, subject to statutory limits. A source credit does not itself grant those permissions. [17 USC 106 and 107](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html#106)
- There is no general safe percentage or word count for fair use. Its application depends on circumstances; changing someone else's protected work is not an automatic authorization to claim it. [Copyright Office permission/fair-use FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-fairuse.html)
- Section 230 expressly preserves intellectual-property law; Fork should not rely on the ordinary user-content publisher defense as a copyright shield. [47 USC 230(e)(2)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-136/t47/s230/)
- A significant current development: **Cox Communications v Sony Music, 25 March 2026**, held that contributory copyright liability requires intent evidenced by inducing infringement or providing a service tailored to infringement. Merely knowing that a general-purpose service will be used to infringe was insufficient. The case concerned internet access and contributory liability; it did not decide that every hosting platform is immune, abolish direct/vicarious claims, or make failing DMCA requirements harmless in every situation. The opinion also confirms that losing safe harbor does not automatically establish infringement. [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-171_bq7d.pdf)

### Inferences

**Practical examples, not adjudications:**

| User action | How Fork should understand it |
| --- | --- |
| Cooks a chef's dish, describes their own experience in original words and posts their own photo | Good intended use. Preserve a source/inspiration link; don't suggest the chef endorses the adaptation. |
| Copies only ingredient quantities and a basic functional process | Lower copyright concern than copying expressive content; claiming original invention can still violate Fork's community attribution rules. |
| Copies distinctive directions, headnotes or the chef's photo, changes the title and claims authorship | Material copyright risk; a new title or small ingredient change does not resolve the copied expression. |
| Copies the entire blog recipe with a credit/link | Attribution helps readers but does not establish permission or fair use. Treat text and photo rights separately. |
| Imports privately, then publishes the imported copy | Private storage is not a blanket copyright exception, and publication deserves a separate, deliberate decision. |

Plagiarism is an authorship/credit problem; copyright infringement concerns protected expression and legal authorization. They overlap but are not identical. Fork can prohibit passing another cook's contribution off as one's own even where a bare ingredient list is uncopyrightable. Conversely, properly credited copying can still infringe. Avoid UI language suggesting that “adapted from” is a legal clearance.

Distinguish **a user's directed upload/import** from **Fork's own editorial library**. If Fork staff copy recipes or images to seed discovery, that is Fork's own content activity, not automatically user-directed hosting. Require actual rights clearance for editorial collections. A user pressing Import is relevant to the hosting analysis, but it is not a magic label: automatic fetching, rewriting, curation, promotion, image copying and publication each need review in the actual implementation. Do not advertise Fork as a way to bypass paid cookbooks or appropriate creators' work.

### Gaps

- No particular recipe, photograph, license, employment agreement or fair-use claim was assessed. The chef may not own all relevant rights; a publisher or photographer may.
- Copyright analysis does not settle trademark, endorsement, publicity, contract, trade-secret or all state-law issues. Those are counsel questions if real facts raise them, not reasons to invent a universal pre-screening requirement.

## 2. What does US safe harbor require, and what does it not promise?

### Takeaway

Treat DMCA safe harbor as a conditional legal defense supported by a working process, not an immunity checkbox. The statute does not require checking every recipe before posting; it also does not permit ignoring relevant knowledge or valid notices while relying on the defense.

### Cited Findings

- Section 512(c) covers qualifying storage at users' direction. Conditions include no disqualifying actual/red-flag knowledge, or expeditious disabling/removal upon awareness; expeditious response to compliant notices; and no directly attributable financial benefit where the provider has the right/ability to control infringement. Section 512(i) requires an adopted, communicated, reasonably implemented repeat-infringer termination policy and accommodation of qualifying standard technical measures. Those measures have statutory consensus, availability and burden criteria—not every vendor's proposed filter. Section 512(m) generally does not condition protection on affirmative monitoring. Protection limits monetary remedies but leaves specified injunctions possible. [17 USC 512(c), (i), (j), (m)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t17/s512/)
- A compliant notice substantially identifies the copyrighted work, locates the challenged material, supplies contact/signature details, and includes good-faith and accuracy/authority statements. After a notice-based removal, promptly notify the uploader. A qualifying counter-notice identifies the removed material, supplies a signature and contact details, states mistake/misidentification under penalty of perjury, and includes jurisdiction/service consent. Forward it promptly to the complainant. The section 512(g) process restores access **10–14 business days after receiving the counter-notice**, unless the agent first receives notice of a filed action seeking a court order restraining the relevant infringement. These procedures support statutory protections, not a platform ruling on ultimate ownership. [Copyright Office section 512 guide](https://www.copyright.gov/512/)
- Designate an agent both publicly on the service and with the Copyright Office; maintain accurate contact information. Hosting firms seeking this defense cannot rely on an email address alone without registration. [Official designated-agent directory](https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/)
- Agent designations expire after three years unless renewed through amendment/resubmission; the current fee is $6. The registration identifies the operator's legal name, address, relevant alternate names/domains and agent contact details. Separate legal entities need separate designations. A role or third-party service can be the agent. [Directory FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/faq.html)

### Inferences

Use a managed inbox and small case record initially; a friends pilot does not need an automated legal department. Capture receipt times, claimed work, exact URLs/assets/versions, uploader identity, actions, notifications and counter-notice dates. A clear manual process beats a sophisticated report button that nobody monitors.

Do not reduce expeditious handling to an invented universal statutory “24-hour rule.” Choose an operational response target and backup coverage that fit the pilot, then have counsel review it. A founder away for weeks with an unattended inbox is a different risk from a small pilot with daily attention.

Separate ordinary attribution reports, DMCA notices and general community moderation. A complaint that “my ingredient list was copied” should not automatically be treated as a proven infringement. Incomplete notices need a documented response path; disputed notices should not become public accusations. Staff should not improvise the legal adequacy of counter-notices, litigation notices or repeated-infringement patterns.

Communicate that counter-notices involve personal contact details and legal statements; do not offer a casual “disagree” button that silently submits one. Keep a separate reason for any independent community-rule restriction. Ask counsel how to handle continued non-copyright moderation when statutory restoration becomes relevant, rather than promising unconditional republication.

Membership fees alone are not a finding that Fork receives the disqualifying infringement-linked benefit; equally, paid access is not a defense. Review actual incentives and promotion. Repeatedly featuring known unauthorized copies to attract subscriptions is materially different from charging for useful cookbook tools.

### Gaps

- This research does not establish that Fork qualifies for any safe harbor. Operator identity, contracts, rights practices, actual knowledge, control, technical behavior and case law applying Cox to hosting must be evaluated.
- The browsed statutory edition for section 512 is current through 15 October 2026; section 230 through 22 October 2026. Sources were accessed 8 November. No claim is made that every subsequent lower-court interpretation was exhaustively reviewed.

## 3. What should Fork build before a friends pilot and before wider social launch?

### Takeaway

An early social pilot is compatible with proportionate rights handling. Build provenance, deliberate publishing and a comprehensive disable-access path early; reserve scalable automation for growth. Append-only history must not mean publicly undeletable infringing content.

### Cited Findings

- The Copyright Office's hosting guidance treats user-directed storage and a registered/public agent as central to section 512(c), and distinguishes safe-harbor loss from an automatic conclusion of liability. [Copyright Office online service providers](https://www.copyright.gov/onlinesp/)
- Statutory notice procedures permit removal or disabling access rather than prescribing a particular database design. [Copyright Act chapter 5](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512)

### Inferences

**Product requirements for the friends pilot:**

1. **Separate author, uploader and source.** Store/display original source URL or book citation, named author when supplied, and who made the Fork adaptation. Preserve source-version lineage in forks. Allow unknown authorship rather than manufacture an attribution. Do not assign “original creator” simply because someone pressed Import.
2. **Ask the meaningful publication question.** Original work, permission/license, or another claimed legal basis should be distinguishable. An attestation records a user's representation; it does not verify rights or transfer another person's rights. Keep imported work private until deliberate publishing, and explain photos need their own permission basis.
3. **Keep source and rights separate.** A URL says where something came from; a license says what reuse is allowed. Record license/permission evidence when provided. Do not automatically apply an open license to copied third-party material. Have counsel define what users authorize Fork and other users to do when they publish/fork.
4. **Enable reports and intervention.** A visible contact/report route, a monitored inbox, a moderation case record, uploader contact, and tested recipe/asset disabling are sufficient starting tools. Provide a manual route for rights reports without requiring the claimant to create a Fork account.
5. **Operate the US process if relying on US safe harbor.** Confirm operator identity; complete the designation/public contact setup; adopt a communicated repeat-infringer policy and actually execute it; prepare notice/counter-notice handling with dates and backups. These are recommended conditions of relying on that defense even in a pilot, not a claim that hosting without registration is itself illegal.

**Removal architecture:** put access/moderation state alongside immutable recipe content. Disable the affected content wherever Fork serves it: live recipe, old versions, diff text, public forks that carry the same material, collection cards, search snippets, image originals/variants, CDN caches, social previews and exports. A latest-version edit alone can leave the complained-of photograph or directions accessible in history. Test direct asset URLs and cached responses, not just the recipe screen.

Use lineage and asset references to locate related copies for review; don't assume every descendant is infringing or erase unrelated original contributions automatically. A notice's scope and actual knowledge determine the appropriate response, with counsel handling uncertainty. Avoid a promise that Fork can retract screenshots or copies previously downloaded outside its control.

For internal history, prefer a minimal restricted audit trail and reversible access suppression while a dispute is processed. A tombstone can preserve that a version existed without continuing to display the challenged material. Counsel should define retention, evidence holds and when content bytes must be deleted; neither append-only engineering preferences nor a blanket “keep everything forever” policy should override legal requirements. Backup restoration must reapply current takedown states.

**Before broader public growth:** rehearse a complete report → disable → notify → counter-notice → restore/litigation-hold case using dummy content. Verify moderation covers every new sharing surface. Assign coverage during absence, measure time to action and unresolved cases, handle repeated evasion/reuploads, and reassess volume before adding automation. Rights-clear any promotional/demo/curated content independently. None of this requires a universal copyright scanner before friends can share their own cooking.

**Focused counsel questions:** Which entity operates Fork and where? How do US safe harbors apply to its actual import/hosting and curated-content flows? What public/forking license is appropriate without overreaching? What repeat-infringer and counter-notice policy fits the service? What evidence/backup retention is lawful? How should US and Canadian processes coexist? Does the chosen operator/contact setup expose Spencer's home address, and what lawful agent/address arrangement is appropriate? Which insurance and escalation arrangements are proportionate when moving from friends to public growth?

### Gaps

- No notice workflow, source fields, agent registration, public policy or takedown coverage was verified in Fork here. These are roadmap requirements to check, not claims of existing defects.
- Canadian copyright, moral rights and intermediary procedures require the separate Canadian analysis; applying a US takedown template globally without review is not the recommendation.

### Follow-up: does a fork become new IP, and can creator partnerships solve permission?

**Short answer:** a fork can contain new copyrightable contributions without transferring ownership of the chef's existing expression. Forking is a product action, not a legal reset. Changing ingredients or technique does not create ownership of the underlying cooking facts, nor remove rights in copied prose/photos. [Copyright Office Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf)

**Legal distinction:** 17 USC **103(b)** limits derivative-work copyright to the contributor's new material, leaving ownership and scope of preexisting copyright unaffected. Section **106(2)** reserves preparation of derivative works to the rightsholder, subject to statutory exceptions. Section **103(a)** excludes protection for portions using preexisting copyrighted material unlawfully. Thus, do not promise blanket enforceable ownership of an unauthorized adaptation; separately original notes or photos require their own analysis. [17 USC 103(a)–(b), 106(2)](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html#103) The Copyright Office explains that protection covers original additions, not the underlying work. [Circular 14](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ14.pdf)

**Creator partnership recommendation:** negotiate a specific permission package, not merely “may we feature your recipe?” Permission to host/display is not necessarily permission for users to adapt, publicly redistribute, download/export, or reuse commercially. For each recipe and image, identify who controls the rights; an authenticated chef account does not establish that the chef controls a publisher's text or photographer's picture. The agreement should specify assets/versions, allowed Fork/user actions, territories, attribution/change marking, monetized use, duration, termination, and what happens to already-authorized forks and exports. These are proposed product/legal questions for counsel, not drafted contract terms.

**Open-license boundaries:** CC BY 4.0 allows sharing and adaptations, including commercial use, subject to conditions such as attribution and change indication; it grants only rights the licensor controls. Its grant is irrevocable while complied with, though breach can terminate rights under its terms. Simply removing the source later does not revoke existing compliant grants. [CC BY 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en) CC BY-ND does not permit distributing adapted material under that license. [CC BY-ND](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/) CC BY-NC restricts commercial uses; don't assume every use on a paid service is either automatically permitted or automatically forbidden without reviewing the actual use. [CC BY-NC](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/) ShareAlike imposes license conditions on distributed adaptations. [CC BY-SA](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/) Custom partnership revocation/termination and survival of prior permissions depend on the agreement; do not promise either retroactive recall or permanent rights by default.

**Operational permission checker (recommendation):** record the evidence, grant version/date, covered text/images and permitted operations. Gate host, public-fork, redistribution and export actions separately where relevant; unknown rights should trigger a reviewed path rather than a fabricated “cleared” label. Preserve attribution and required license notices downstream. Model rights assertions separately from verification: an identity badge means the account was authenticated; a permission record means a particular grant was checked within a stated scope. Neither guarantees every legal requirement is satisfied. Retain a rights-dispute path regardless of badges.

**Gap:** no actual partnership, disputed fork, license compatibility or rights chain has been assessed. There is no ingredient-change percentage that answers these questions.
