# Research: Cooklang, and what Fork should take from it

2026-10-23. Reviewer analysis of Cooklang as a competitor and a source of ideas, with the conclusions tied to Fork's roadmap. Claims about Cooklang come from its public documentation (cooklang.org, cook.md), not from hands-on testing; where the reviewer did not find something, that is noted as such rather than as proof it does not exist.

## Summary

Cooklang is one of the closest conceptual competitors to Fork and one of the best projects to learn from. Borrow its treatment of recipe structure and portability; do not turn Fork into another Cooklang editor.

The distinction to pursue:

> Cooklang organizes recipes as programmable documents. Fork should specialize in helping cooks develop recipes: adapt them, cook them, record what happened, and make the next version better.

That is a positioning opportunity, not an advantage already established. Cooklang's ecosystem overlaps with more of Fork's plan than expected, including AI that edits recipe files with visible changes and undo, and a dated meal journal. "AI that modifies the recipe rather than chatting" and "logging what you cooked" are therefore not unique claims.

## How this ties to the roadmap

| Roadmap item | What this research says |
| --- | --- |
| One save, versioned formulation (`specs/2026-10-23-one-save-design.md`) | Finish it. Cooklang gives no reason to interrupt; complete snapshots and one coherent save are what make version history trustworthy. |
| Serving scaling (next after one save) | Keep the three layers separate: exact quantity arithmetic, practical measurement guidance, process adaptations proposed separately. A temporary multiplier never writes a version. Fixed quantities should be an explicit author choice, not an inferred rule that salt stays fixed. |
| First AI adaptation | Benchmark against CookBot on the same recipes before building. The standard: preserves intent, updates dependent instructions, explains trade-offs, cooks better. Grounded in the selected version and real outcomes. |
| M11 step timers | Timers are recipe content ("this step calls for six minutes"); a running timer is session state. Starting or extending one never rewrites the recipe. |
| M12, M13 gram tables | Deprioritise expanding tables relative to connecting one adaptation to one real cooking attempt. |
| M15 importing | Add `.cook` import as a stage after the core loop works. Preserve the original file, translate the supported subset, and declare what is not represented. Evaluate existing TypeScript parsers and Cooklang's canonical parser tests before writing a parser. |
| New: export | Portability should be a product guarantee. Target a documented archive: readable recipes, machine-readable snapshots, source attribution, images, versions, attempt notes. Never lock history behind continued payment. |

## 1. Cooklang is more than a format

Three things to distinguish:

- The language: plain-text `.cook` files with explicit ingredients, quantities, equipment, timers, sections and metadata.
- The ecosystem: mobile apps, a desktop editor, command-line tools, self-hosting, integrations.
- The commercial product: Cook Cloud and CookBot, with synchronization, imports, nutrition and AI assistance. The file-based tools are usable offline without an account.

CookBot works on recipe files, shows changes and lets users undo them, and can make changes across a collection. The Meal Journal plugin keeps dated journal files with meal sections and recipe references.

### The comparison that matters

| Dimension | Cooklang's documented approach | Fork's potential direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Writing | Explicit markup with highlighting, autocomplete, live preview | Ordinary recipe text first; structured assistance without requiring markup |
| Recipe structure | Ingredients and timers appear inside instructions | Separate ingredients and method today; explicit links later |
| History | Plain files, Git-based history, branching, collaboration | Revisions, comparisons and variations presented in cooking terms |
| AI | Recipe-file edits, planning, visible changes, undo | Coherent adaptations grounded in the selected version and actual outcomes |
| Cooking records | Meal Journal with dated entries and recipe references | Attempts tied to exact versions, batch sizes, deviations, outcomes |
| Ownership | Local, portable files | Hosted convenience with useful export; no subscription needed to keep recipes |

The Fork column is the opportunity, not a list of completed features.

## 2. Where Fork could have an edge

### A. A recipe-development workflow, not a collection

Organize the experience around: which version did I cook last time, what did I actually change, why did I prefer that result, what should I try next. For example:

> "I cooked version 3 at half size, substituted oat milk, and found it too thin. Make a new version that addresses that without changing the flavor."

A good response distinguishes the saved formulation, the actual attempt and a proposed revision. It does not mistake an improvised substitution for a permanent change, or describe an untested idea as known to work.

The reviewer did not find this version-pinned, deviation-aware loop in Cooklang's materials. That is a more specific target than "we have notes". The hard part is making recording effortless.

Product promise: **"Make your recipes better every time you cook them."**

### B. History without managing Git

Cooklang promotes Git for change tracking, variants and collaboration. Fork offers the result without repositories or branches:

> Original, then my adaptation, then the version I cooked, then the version I prefer.

"Stored in Postgres rather than Git" is not an advantage, and neither is having versions. The question is whether someone can answer "what changed, and did it help?" more easily. The current schema's versions and parent lineage, plus complete snapshots and one coherent save, are the right next step.

### C. Natural writing with optional structure

The two-textarea editors fit this: write normally, get help progressively. A Cooklang instruction makes ingredient, preparation, equipment and duration explicit:

```cooklang
Sauté @onion{1}(finely diced) in @olive oil{2%tbsp}
using a #skillet for ~{6%minutes}.
```

Fork lets someone write "Sauté the diced onion in 2 tbsp olive oil for 6 minutes" and then offers a linked ingredient or timer. Implicit structure is harder to infer than explicit markup; the ingredient normalisation work showed this. Preserve ambiguous text and ask for confirmation rather than pretending a sentence is unambiguous. CookBot can generate Cooklang for users, so "no curly braces" is a UX goal, not a moat.

### D. Cooking-specific adaptation quality

Standard for an adaptation: preserves intent, updates all dependent instructions, explains consequential trade-offs, produces a better result when cooked. Convincing examples: adapting equipment while preserving the dish; revising a repeatedly disappointing recipe using the user's notes. Unconvincing: generating another attractive recipe from scratch. Test culinary results and correction effort, not formatting.

## 3. Ideas to borrow

**Ingredient usage belongs in the instructions.** The most valuable technical idea. Cooklang's cooking mode shows the ingredients for the current step. Fork's `Step` has text and an optional annotation; ingredients are separate. Eventually a step references an ingredient and the amount used there. Add when a feature needs it.

**Timers are recipe content; running timers are session state.** See M11.

**Scaling is explicit, with exceptions visible.** Cooklang distinguishes proportional scaling, fixed quantities, timers and cookware, and servings from measurable yield. Borrow the fixed-quantity flag as an explicit choice.

**Subrecipes are usable components**, referenced by version.

**Portability is a product guarantee.** Prove ownership through export, not a sentence in the terms.

## 4. Should Fork adopt Cooklang?

| Option | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Borrow concepts and test cases | Yes, now |
| Import and export `.cook` files | A bounded addition after the core cooking loop works |
| Rebuild Fork around `.cook` as the sole source of truth | No demonstrated need |

The mismatch: Cooklang carries ingredient occurrences inside the method; Fork stores an ingredient list beside ordinary step text. Conversion is not always lossless. An importer preserves the original and translates the supported subset; an exporter must not invent ingredient-to-step links to produce impressive markup. "Cooklang-compatible" needs a tested definition.

Avoid growing a Fork-specific markup piecemeal. `tip:` is manageable. Once ingredients, equipment, timers, component references and optional items all need syntax, reconsider an established format or a visual structured editor.

## 5. Where not to compete

Not "match the whole Cooklang ecosystem": photo and library imports, planning, shopping lists, sync, nutrition. Not the user whose requirement is local files managed in Git; respect them through export. Target instead:

> "I cook regularly, change recipes, and want an excellent workspace that helps me understand and improve the results, without maintaining a technical system myself."

A hypothesis to test, not a claim Cooklang cannot serve it.

## 6. Immediate implications

1. Finish the one-save, versioned-formulation work.
2. Then connect one reviewed adaptation to one real cooking attempt, ahead of expanding gram tables or matching organizer features.
3. Before the next AI build, run the same recipes through Cook Editor and CookBot: enter or import, adapt under a constraint, review changes, cook, record a deviation and outcome, revise again. Measure corrections, lost context, and whether the second revision benefits from the first cook.

Conclusion: Cooklang strengthens the case for structured, portable recipes and raises the bar. Do not compete on a "recipes + AI + version history" checklist. The edge is an unusually good improvement loop: clear versions, faithful adaptations, low-effort cooking records, advice grounded in what happened.
